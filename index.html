Project Overview

Project RAPTOR (Rocket Apogee Payload Transition to Optimal Return) is an electrical engineering capstone project conducted over two full academic semesters (Fall 2026 through Spring 2027). Our five-member Electrical Engineering team aims to integrate an autonomous fixed-wing UAV payload deployment system within a custom high-powered rocket launch vehicle. At a target apogee of 1,500 to 2,000 ft. AGL, an electronically actuated CO2 cold-gas canister ejects the nosecone and the RAPTOR Mini UAV. Upon separation, the UAV rotates its 30-inch wing into flight orientation and executes actively stabilized, controlled glide flight back to a designated recovery area while the rocket recovers safely under dual-deployment parachutes. This platform provides a cost-effective solution for rapid response and high-altitude imaging.

Figure 1 depicts the complete mission flight profile, illustrating the RAPTOR Mega launch to apogee, pneumatic payload separation, RAPTOR Mini descent and independent recoveries for both vehicles.

Apogee Deployment & Separation Sequence

At Apogee, the RAPTOR Mega Rocket Module, The RAPTOR Mini UAV Module, and the Payload Ejection Module will simultaneously operate to carry out the project goal. Shortly after deployment, the RAPTOR Mega module will separate into three sections including the nose cone, payload bay, and motor bay. This will be followed by a parachute recovery deployment as The RAPTOR Mini completes the remainder of the mission.

Airframe Design Evolution (Version 0 to Version 2)

Version 0 - Initial Proof of Concept | Proof-of-Concept CAD & Spring Wing Mechanism
Proof-of-Concept Baseline & Spring Wing Test

Version 0 was our initial proof of concept to set the 30.0-inch wing geometry and make sure the folded drone would fit inside the 7.51-inch rocket airframe. The early Version 0 CAD used metal thrust ball bearings in the central wing pivot to test rotation stops and clearances, This shows that we can use a torsion spring to snap the wings open when it launches out of the rocket.

Version 1 - Full-Scale 3D Printed Prototype & Nylon Washer System | Physical Prototype & Baseline Airflow Testing
Dihedral Winglet Integration & Nylon Washer Pivot

Version 1 added upturned winglets to reduce wingtip drag during glide. To stop bearing binding, save weight, and make deployment more reliable, we replaced the heavy metal ball bearings from Version 0 with a low-friction nylon washer swivel system. We 3D printed full-scale prototypes to check wing rotation stops, spar fit, and stowed clearance inside the 7.51-inch launch tube.

Version 2 - Current CAD with Cooling Vents & Control Surfaces | Simulated in ANSYS CFD & Ready for ASA Aero Printing
Version 2 wing cross-section view showing internal channels for the carbon fiber reinforcement rods.
Avionics Cooling Vents, Nylon Washer Pivot & Ailerons

Version 2 is our current CAD iteration with front NACA-style cooling vents to keep the onboard electronics and video transmitter cool, the nylon washer swivel system, winglets, and trailing-edge ailerons. The wing cross-section above also shows the internal channels where the carbon fiber rods run through the wing to add stiffness.

Materials, Bambu Lab ASA Aero & Propulsion Integration

Our initial prototype was printed in standard PETG to check mechanical clearances, which is too heavy for flight. For our flight airframe, we are printing the glider out of Bambu Lab ASA Aero active-foaming filament with carbon-fiber structural spars:

  • Bambu Lab ASA Aero Active-Foaming Filament: Made for lightweight UAV and RC aircraft, ASA Aero foams as it extrudes at higher nozzle temperatures. Tuning print temperature and flow rate cuts airframe weight by roughly 50% to 60% compared to solid filaments while keeping the outer shape accurate.
  • UV, Weather & Heat Resistance: Unlike PLA or LW-PLA, which can warp in the sun, ASA Aero holds up well in UV light and desert heat at the launch site.
  • Carbon Fiber Spar Backbone: Continuous carbon fiber rods run down the centerline of the fuselage and through the main wing root (shown in the V2 section view) to handle bending loads during deployment and pull-out.
  • Rear Pusher Propeller System: A rear-mounted brushless pusher motor with folding propeller blades provides forward thrust for controlled return to the recovery zone.

Avionics Schematic & BOM

The Avionics for the RAPTOR Mini center on the MATEK Mateksys F405 Wing V2 Flight Controller. This flight controller includes an IMU, which is essential for Autonomous and Assisted Flight. We chose this flight controller for its abundance of PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) channels, I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit) channels, and software support. Looking at the software side, this will be running ArduPlane, which is a variant of ArduPilot built for fixed-wing planes. The ArduPlane autopilot and assisted flight are built around multiple separate PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) controllers nested inside a programmatic loop. The current plan is to test this setup on the ground; if, during these tests, this controller is inadequate, then custom controllers can be implemented in C within ArduPilot.

  • Communications: To communicate with RAPTOR Mini, a 2.4 GHz Boxer Radio Controller will be used to transmit steering inputs and commands to a 2.4 GHz ELRS (ExpressLRS) receiver, and a separate 30 dBm 5.725-5.850 GHz, 40 MHz bandwidth, 1080p video transmitter that can embed telemetry data to the ground station. Once these components arrive, range tests can begin.

RAPTOR Mini V2 and V1 Simulations

Our team completed CFD simulations using ANSYS Student of both V2 and V1 on the RAPTOR Mini and then calculated the difference in efficiency from V1 to V2. Before we printed the RAPTOR Mini V2 out of ASA Aero we did a CFD simulation on the design and then graphed the results with MATLAB. We used ANSYS to simulate lift force, drag force, different contours and different path lines on the drone. We did simulations from 10 mph to 50 mph and at an angle of attack of 4 degrees and at 12 degrees.

RAPTOR Mini Version 2

For the RAPTOR Mini V2 we could not mesh the CAD shape with the front vents and the ailerons, so we had to make the overall shape more simple.

Below we made a gif of the path lines using the single pulse setting in ANSYS. This creates a video of how the air reacts around RAPTOR Mini V2.

Figure: Animated pathline pulses across the RAPTOR Mini V2

After getting the data from ANSYS we graphed the results in MATLAB below. On the left is the V2 lift vs speed, and we can see this grow exponentially. Then in the middle is the same but with drag and notice how the 12 AOA gets more as the speed increases. Then at the right is the efficiency and that's lift over drag, as we can see that at 4 degrees the efficiency is substantially higher than the 12 degree angle of attack. At a 30 mph cruise angle the glider makes 3.7 N of lift force and that can lift a glider that has a mass of 377 grams.

Visual Airflow Images

Next we captured 6 post processing CFD images to show how the RAPTOR Mini V2 generates lift and glides.

1. Velocity pathline ribbons

On the V2 we have velocity ribbons that are color coded tracking the air acceleration around the V2. We can see the air glide smoothly over the V2 main wing, tail wings and the fuselage.

2. Lift Generation: Upper Wing Suction

This shows the pressure contour on the V2 when it's flying. With the pressure contour we can actually see low pressure on top of the main wing with blue color on the top. This means that the wing is generating lift with lower pressure air on top of the plane and higher-pressure air on the bottom of the plane.

3. Lift Generation: Underside Upward Push

Now we turned the plane to see the underside with the same contour, and we can see most of the V2 is green and yellow. This means that there is a higher pressure (green and yellow) on the bottom of the V2 compared to the top (blue and cyan).

4. Stability: Centerline Velocity Magnitude contour

This post CFD shows the velocity magnitude right down the middle of the V2 with the mesh still drawn in. This shows the low velocity at the tail end with the blue to green transition contour. Also, there is a higher velocity magnitude around the transition from the nose to the fuselage.

5. Drag: Skin Friction & Wall Shear Stress

This shows the skin friction from the air moving across V2 in units of pressure. The contour shows red for the highest shear stress and blue for the least amount. We can see the nose and the back fuselage being blue, which means not as much air is flying past those sections as the top of the main wing.

6. Pressure Pathlines

Pressure pathlines over the RAPTOR Mini V2, with blue representing low pressure and red highlighting the high pressure. We can see the green pathlines past the wings showing that the pressure is normal after the main wing.

Custom 7.51-Inch High-Powered Rocket

Because housing a 30-inch-wingspan drone exceeds standard kit rockets, the team built a custom high-power rocket airframe. The vehicle has a 7.51-inch outer diameter (with a 20.0-inch total fin span) and stands 110.0 inches (9.17 feet) tall. It is designed to carry the RAPTOR Mini to an apogee of 1,500 to 2,000 ft AGL and recover safely under dual-deployment parachutes.

Custom Composite Layup & Laser-Cut Aerodynamic Fins

While commercial 7.5-inch rocket kits are sold off the shelf, our team chose not to buy a pre-made kit. To customize our internal payload bay dimensions and stay on budget, we ordered standalone 7.51-inch heavy kraft cardboard tubes that we reinforced ourselves using wet-layup fiberglass fabric and epoxy resin.

Laser-Cut Composite Fins: For the fins, we used a laser cutter to cut thin wood cores and then laid fiberglass sheets over the wood with epoxy. This composite sandwich construction gives us stiff, lightweight fins that can handle launch thrust and aerodynamic loads.

Launch Site Operations: Tripoli Phoenix (TRAPHX) Eagle Eye Site

Flight certification and full-scale mission launches for Project RAPTOR take place with the Tripoli Phoenix Rocketry Association (TRAPHX Prefecture #67) at their dedicated Eagle Eye Launch Site located off Eagle Eye Road near Aguila and Wickenburg, Arizona.

The site has an FAA airspace waiver up to 48,000 ft AGL (50,000 ft MSL) and provides a wide-open desert recovery area. This open range gives plenty of room for booster parachute recovery and autonomous waypoint return of the RAPTOR Mini glider. For launch schedules, rules, and club info, visit https://traphx.clubexpress.com/.

Building Progress: Fiberglass Airframe Layup, Alignment Mounts & Fin Fabrication

In early November 2026, the team began full-scale composite manufacturing of the 7.51-inch rocket airframe. To apply a uniform fiberglass layup over the 54-inch and 30-inch heavy kraft body tubes, we built a horizontal PVC pipe rotation mandrel supported on sawhorses over a protective tarp.

3D Printed Alignment Mounts for Concentric Drying: To keep the heavy composite airframe straight and concentric without sagging under wet resin weight, we 3D printed internal spoke-hub rotation mounts. These adapters clamped onto the rotating PVC mandrel pipe and pressed evenly against the inside tube wall so the fiberglass and two-part epoxy cured straight. We also began cutting, clamping, and assembling the wooden fin cores prior to fiberglass layup.
